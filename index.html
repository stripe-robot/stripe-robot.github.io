Abstract

We can reduce the cost of evaluating robot policies by leveraging simulation. For this, we present a real-to-sim policy evaluation framework that combines mesh-based scene reconstruction, calibrated robot dynamics, and learned image-to-image translation. We reconstruct the workspace, robot, and manipulated objects in MuJoCo, calibrate robot dynamics against recorded real trajectories, and integrate STRIPE, our Sim-to-real Image Translation for Policy Evaluation model, into the observation pipeline. A single translator shared across tasks and camera views adapts simulation rendered observations to real-camera appearance while preserving the spatial layout of the input image, then provides these observations to the policy during closed-loop evaluation. Across four manipulation tasks and four policy families, our framework with STRIPE achieves stronger aggregate success-rate correlation and policy rank agreement than variants using raw simulation images, classical color alignment, or a simpler translation backbone. Adding a Gaussian-splatting background provides no further improvement in policy agreement. These results demonstrate that integrating learned image translation with mesh-based simulation and calibrated dynamics supports real-to-sim policy comparison without requiring photorealistic scene reconstruction. Finally, loss ablations show that closer appearance matching alone does not ensure better policy agreement, highlighting the need to retain task-relevant spatial layout during translation.

Method

1

Mesh-based scene

The workspace, the robot (from its CAD model), and the manipulated objects are rigid bodies with explicit collision geometry in MuJoCo. Cameras are calibrated to the real side and wrist cameras, and each episode's initial object poses are aligned to the first real frame with a SAM mask overlay.

2

Calibrated dynamics

We replay recorded real actions in simulation and fit per-joint PD stiffness and damping with CMA-ES, minimizing the gap between simulated and real joint trajectories plus a small actuator-effort penalty.

3

STRIPE translation

An image2image-turbo translator (frozen SD-Turbo with LoRA adapters) maps every rendered frame to real-camera appearance. One model serves all tasks and both cameras, trained on 1,165 sim/real pairs.

Change the appearance. Keep the scene.

STRIPE learns to make simulated images look like real camera images while keeping objects where they are. Training balances two goals:

Make it look real. Learn the appearance of real camera images, including color, lighting, and texture.

Keep objects in place. Compare each translated image with its original simulation render to preserve object positions and boundaries.

Why this matters: real and simulated training images are not perfectly aligned. The original render anchors the scene's layout, so matching the real image's appearance does not mean copying its object positions.

Training details

We combine an adversarial loss for real-camera appearance with a DINOv3 patch-feature consistency loss between each translated image and its own raw simulation image for spatial layout.

𝓛(φ) = λadv 𝓛adv + λdino 𝓛dino

Real-camera appearance, with object layout preserved

STRIPE recovers shading and color while preserving object shapes and positions. Classical Color Alignment leaves the scene flat, and Pix2Pix introduces blur and distorts the spatial layout of the robot and objects.

STRIPE better preserves policy rankings

Averaged across four tasks, STRIPE gives stronger agreement with real-world policy performance than the visual baselines. We train ACT, Diffusion Policy, GR00T N1.5 and π0.5, take four checkpoints of each, and evaluate all 16 checkpoints on four tasks in the real world and in simulation. Agreement is measured with MMRV (mean maximum rank violation, lower is better) and Pearson r (higher is better).

What matters for policy agreement

Finding 1 Gaussian splatting adds no improvement here

Prior real-to-sim evaluation pipelines rely on Gaussian-splatting backgrounds for realism. With STRIPE in the loop, replacing our mesh-only background with a Gaussian-splatting one does not improve agreement. STRIPE trained on either input recovers similar texture, shading and color, so the extra reconstruction and registration effort can be skipped.

Finding 2 Matching brightness drives most of the gain

To separate the two visual properties STRIPE changes, we revert one at a time in CIELAB space. Lightness Swap restores the raw simulation image's brightness and collapses to raw simulation performance; Color Swap restores the raw simulation image's color and keeps most of the gain.
